How RadioBeat works

How it works,
in three steps.

01

Send.

An ESP32 fills the room with a steady WiFi signal. The receiver pings it 100 times a second to keep the signal flowing.

02

Sense.

An M5StickC reads the signal's Channel State Information: 64 channels, about 40 times a second. Every breath and heartbeat bends it very slightly.

03

Detect.

My Python program removes the room and the breathing, finds the heartbeat, then checks the gaps between beats for signs of AFib.

The idea

Focused ultrasound.

A scanner sends sound in to look around. Curve the transducer instead, and every part of the wave arrives at one point at the same moment. The tissue on the way in stays cool. The focal spot, a few centimetres deep and a few millimetres across, gets hot enough to treat. There is no cut and no needle.

Most people know ultrasound as a way to see inside the body. What I find more interesting is that it can also treat things. Hospitals already do this. Focused ultrasound is used on essential tremor and on some tumours, and in trials it can open the blood brain barrier just long enough to let a drug through.

I want to build the whole chain myself, from the bottom up. Start with the physics, then a signal generator, then something realistic to aim it at, then the focused beam, and last a simulation to check whether my measurements mean anything at all. Five steps, roughly one a year.

  1. 01Now to 2027

    Learn the sound.

    Before building anything I need the physics properly, not just the exam version of it: wavelength, acoustic impedance, why sound reflects at a boundary, how much a beam loses on the way through tissue, and how a curved face turns a flat wave into a point.

    • A learning journal, written up with my own diagrams after each session
    • A pulse oximeter on an Arduino: red and infrared light through a fingertip, heart rate drawn live in Python
    • It measures the same thing as RadioBeat but with totally different physics, which is exactly why I want to build it
    ArduinoMAX30102Pythonmatplotlib
  2. 022027

    Make the sound.

    A generator that puts out a clean, controllable ultrasound signal. Therapeutic work sits roughly between 1 and 5 MHz, far above anything I have driven before, so this is where my electronics has to get serious.

    • An AD9833 direct digital synthesis module, set over serial, feeding an amplifier stage
    • The schematic drawn properly in KiCad instead of on paper
    • Python on the laptop to set the frequency and draw the output waveform
    • An enclosure modelled in Fusion 360 and printed on the P1S
    KiCadAD9833ArduinoPythonFusion 360
  3. 03Early 2028

    Build something to aim at.

    You cannot test on a person, and water is too easy. Phantoms are gels mixed so that sound travels through them at close to the speed it travels through human tissue, which is what makes a test mean anything.

    • Moulds designed in Fusion 360, with slots for wire and bead targets set inside the gel
    • Gelatin and agar recipes, cast, cured, and photographed at every stage that goes wrong
    • Speed of sound measured through each phantom and compared with published tissue values, so I know how honest the phantom is
    • A DICOM viewer in Python, to open a real scan, scroll it, and measure distances using the pixel spacing stored in the file itself
    Fusion 360Agar & PVA gelspydicomPython
  4. 04Late 2028

    Aim it.

    The real build. A single element focused transducer needs more than a signal. It needs power, and it needs the electronics matched to the element, or most of that power reflects straight back into the amplifier instead of going into the water.

    • A power amplifier and an impedance matching network, on a fabricated PCB rather than a breadboard
    • A housing printed to hold the element at exactly its focal distance, with a water coupling face
    • A hydrophone on a Raspberry Pi ADC, moved point by point to map pressure against position
    • Actually measuring where the focal spot lands, instead of just assuming I know
    KiCadPCB fabRaspberry PiHydrophone
  5. 052029

    Check the maths.

    One measurement could be a fluke, and a simulation could just be wrong. I only trust them when they agree. I learned this on RadioBeat, which doesn't show a heart rate unless two different methods agree to within 12%.

    • The same transducer modelled in k-Wave, giving 2D pressure maps of the focal zone
    • Simulation laid over measurement and compared on three numbers: focal distance, −6 dB beam width, peak pressure
    • If they disagree, finding out why is the interesting part
    k-WavePythonNumPy
Why it matters

What focused sound
can already do.

Heat one spot.

Hold the focus still and the temperature there climbs far enough to destroy tissue, while everything around it is left alone. This is how focused ultrasound treats tumours and tremor today.

Nudge a brain circuit.

Short, low-power bursts through the skull can calm down or wake up a small part of the brain. There are no electrodes and no surgery, and the effect wears off.

Drop a drug in one place.

A drug carried inside a capsule that only opens where the beam lands, so the dose goes to the tumour instead of the whole body.

The honest version. All of these are near-future plans, not built yet. The dates are my best guess, and they could easily slip. When I do build them, the electronics, the code, the CAD and the write-ups will be my own work, and I will always say clearly which parts came from someone else.

Mirrored Tree

by Adhvik Mahesh · 2026

Withered leaves, hang down the fallen tree. Once a tall pillar, Now a fallen mirror.

The mortal lies, rehearsing their deceit. Their shadows passing the corner, necks don't turn.

Wine from the elder tree, rotten. The lifeless woods a sour canvas.

Nature does not stay inert, Earth cracking, Water tearing through buildings, Wind hurling dust.

The fallen mirror, stare of the mortals. Waiting for the rust to answer, while the forest drowns in grit.

Themes

  • DecayA tall tree has fallen, its leaves are withered and the wine from it has gone rotten.
  • DeceptionThe mortals rehearse their lies, even while everything around them falls apart.
  • IndifferenceTheir shadows pass right by and nobody turns to look.
  • Nature fighting backNature does not stay quiet. The earth cracks, water tears through buildings and wind throws dust.
  • ReflectionThe fallen tree becomes a mirror, so people are left staring at what they caused.

RadioBeat.

Watching your heartbeat through the air, using only WiFi.

0things to wear
±0.5breaths/min accuracy
2.7BPM median heart-rate error*
6weeks to build

The problem

Atrial Fibrillation (AFib) is an irregular heartbeat that can cause strokes. It is often silent, comes and goes, and usually happens while you sleep, which is exactly when nobody is checking. Every device that could catch it has to be worn, and most people take their smartwatch off at night.

How can I keep track of someone's heartbeat all through the night, and warn them if it becomes dangerously irregular, without them having to wear or touch anything at all?

How it works

Every breath and heartbeat moves your chest a tiny amount, and that changes how WiFi signals travel across a room. One ESP32 sends a steady WiFi signal, and an M5StickC Plus2 reads its Channel State Information (64 channels, about 40 times a second). My Python program then:

  • Cleans the radio's own noise and removes reflections from the room
  • Finds breathing (7 to 36 breaths a minute), then subtracts it and its echoes
  • Averages over four minutes using only channels that agree, to find the heartbeat
  • Measures the gaps between beats (RMSSD, pNN50, entropy) to spot AFib
  • Only warns if five separate checks all pass, to avoid false alarms

What it can do

  • Measure breathing rate with nothing worn and nothing touching the person
  • Estimate heart rate (clearly labelled experimental)
  • Flag an irregular rhythm, and refuse to judge when the signal is too noisy
  • Count how many people are breathing in range
  • Ignore fans and air conditioning automatically
  • Run as a web app with a live view, recorded sessions and optional accounts

Being honest

Breathing is reliable. The heartbeat is at the edge of what this hardware can do: it picks up movement down to about 1 mm, and a heartbeat moves the chest about 0.45 mm. *The accuracy numbers come from test signals with a known answer, not yet from real people. RadioBeat cannot replace an ECG or diagnose anything. It can only suggest that someone should get checked.

Robotics · AI

Reachy Study Buddy.

My Reachy Mini desk robot, turned into a voice tutor that listens, sees and moves.

What it does

  • Listens to what I say and replies out loud
  • Reading mode: I read whole textbook chapters aloud and it quizzes me on what I just read
  • Looks at worksheet questions through its camera
  • Follows my face and moves its head, antennas and base to stay lively
  • Shows emotions with its antennas: wagging when excited, drooping when sad
  • Understands plain movement commands like “look up” or “dance”

How it's built

The brain is a local AI model (Qwen, running through Ollama), so it's free and nothing leaves my laptop. Whisper turns speech into text on the GPU, and Piper speaks the replies. Face tracking uses a calibrated camera model so the head follows smoothly.

Android

Adhvik's Translator.

An Android app that translates any text on my screen into English, live.

What it does

  • Translates Spanish, Malay, Russian, Korean and Japanese on screen as it appears
  • A floating on/off button I can park anywhere, plus a Quick Settings tile
  • Choose one area of the screen to translate per app
  • Understands romanized Hindi and Russian typed in English letters
  • Everything runs on the phone: OCR and translation never leave the device

How it's built

It's an Android accessibility service. Google ML Kit reads Latin, Korean and Japanese text, and Tesseract reads Russian (Cyrillic), which ML Kit can't. Labels are tracked between frames so they don't flicker.

Statistics

IQ Lab.

A set of thinking tests I built to see how my scores change over time. One score on its own does not say much.

How it works

  • Five areas: reasoning, knowledge, visual, working memory and speed
  • Every puzzle is generated fresh by code
  • Scored with item response theory, with age norms
  • Tracks the trend from one sitting to the next
  • Scripts that test the scoring by simulation and check every puzzle can be solved

There's no official standardisation sample, so the absolute score might be a few points off. That offset stays the same every time, which is why the trend is the part to trust.

Periodic Table.

An interactive table of all 118 elements that explains why each one sits where it does.

Try it live

What it does

  • Click any of the 118 elements to open its details
  • Atomic number, mass, and the number of protons, neutrons and electrons
  • Group, period and block
  • Electron configuration and electron shells
  • A written explanation of why the element belongs to its family, like why it is a noble gas or a metalloid
  • Tiles show the rounded mass, and the detail panel shows the exact value

I built it while studying chemistry, because knowing why an element is in its group is what actually helps in exams.
